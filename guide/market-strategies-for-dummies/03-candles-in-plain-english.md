## At a glance

A candlestick is a **finished time box**. It tells you where price started, where it ended, and how far it stretched while that box was forming. It is the alphabet of chart reading.

## Why candles matter

Before you can understand fair value gaps, sweeps, wedges, or trend, you need to become fluent in what one finished candle is saying. Most beginners skip this step and jump straight to pattern names. That creates a fake sense of skill. If you cannot calmly describe a single candle, you are not ready to trust a three-candle setup.

Candles matter because they answer three basic questions:

1. Where did price **open**?
2. Where did price **close**?
3. How far did price explore **above** and **below** that box before settling?

That is already enough to tell you whether the market looked decisive, hesitant, rejected, or quiet.

## One candle = one time box

Each candle summarizes what happened during a chunk of time (1m, 5m, 1h, 1d, …):

| Part | Meaning | What a beginner should notice |
|---|---|---|
| **Open** | First price in the box | Where the story began |
| **Close** | Last price in the box | Who “won” the box |
| **High** | Highest price tagged | How far buyers pushed |
| **Low** | Lowest price tagged | How far sellers pushed |
| **Body** | Between open and close | Conviction / net progress |
| **Wicks** | The shadows beyond the body | Rejection, testing, or noise |

If close is above open, buyers “won the box” (often colored green). If close is below open, sellers won (often red). Colors are cosmetic; **close vs open** is the fact.

## How to read candle personality

### Big body, small wicks

Usually means one side had control for most of the box. This does **not** automatically mean the next candle follows through, but it does mean the market was not indecisive during that period.

### Tiny body, long wicks

Usually means argument, not clarity. Price traveled, but neither side kept control. This often happens inside messy areas where beginners overtrade.

### Long lower wick

Price traded lower, but by the close buyers pushed it back up. This can become useful later near a level, but only if the larger context supports it.

### Long upper wick

Mirror image: price traded higher, but sellers pushed it back down before the box closed.

## What a candle does *not* tell you

- Who “institutions” were  
- Whether the next candle will reverse  
- Whether you should be all-in (never)  
- Whether a setup is valid without context

A candle is a **summary**, not a prophecy.

## Closed candles only

For learning with this project’s philosophy: wait until the candle **finishes**. Drawing conclusions on a half-formed candle is how people lie to themselves.

That rule matters because a candle can look amazing halfway through and pathetic by the close. Builders designed the platform around **closed-candle evidence** for exactly this reason: you want observations that can be reproduced later.

## Famous names (optional)

You’ll hear “doji,” “engulfing,” “hammer.” Treat names as nicknames. Nice for journaling; not a full strategy.

A good learner sentence is:

> “This was a small-bodied candle with a long lower wick near support.”

That is already better than:

> “Hammer, must bounce.”

## Common beginner mistakes

- Falling in love with one candle before the box closes  
- Treating every long wick like a reversal signal  
- Ignoring where the candle formed  
- Using candle names as automatic trade buttons

## Practice

On any liquid chart (BTC, ETH, a major FX pair, etc.):

1. Pick the 15-minute chart.  
2. Screenshot 20 closed candles.  
3. Label 5 of them: “long wick up,” “long wick down,” “big body,” “tiny body.”  
4. Write one sentence: “Most of these boxes look like ___ (trend / chop / mixed).”
5. For two candles, explain them without using any pattern nickname.

## Builder note

When learners describe candles in plain language, builders can compare those descriptions to future detector outputs. That creates cleaner supervision than vague labels like “looks bullish.”
