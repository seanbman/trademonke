## At a glance

Patterns are **shape nicknames**. They help you describe structure quickly, but they do not replace context or confirmation.

## Patterns are cartoons of structure

A chart pattern is a nickname for a shape made of swings. Useful for **communication**. Dangerous as a standalone trigger.

This project’s knowledge graph includes many patterns from cheat sheets — including **rising wedge** and **falling wedge**.

## A tiny pattern kit

| Pattern | Rough look | Beginner use |
|---|---|---|
| **Flag / pennant** | Sharp move, then tight pause | Hint of continuation *if* context agrees |
| **Ascending triangle** | Flat top, rising lows | Compression into resistance — wait for close + confirm |
| **Descending triangle** | Flat bottom, falling highs | Mirror of above |
| **Rising wedge** | Both lines rise, narrowing | Often a “careful / exhaustion?” hint — **not** auto-short |
| **Falling wedge** | Both lines fall, narrowing | Often a “compression in a decline” hint — **not** auto-long |
| **Double top / bottom** | Two similar highs/lows | Level cluster story |

## The rule that saves accounts

> Pattern = **optional tag** on question 2 (location/structure).  
> It never replaces questions 1 and 3.

If you only remember one sentence from this lesson, remember that.

## Why wedges get people in trouble

Wedges feel smart because the lines look clean.

But a wedge by itself does not tell you:

- whether the bigger chart is trending or chopping  
- whether the wedge is forming at a meaningful level  
- whether the breakout/reversal actually confirmed on a close

That is why wedges are in this course as **visual aids**, not as holy grails.

## How to use a pattern responsibly

1. Spot the shape.  
2. Treat it as a **note**, not a signal.  
3. Force the three questions anyway.  
4. If the pattern and the context disagree, trust context more.

## Common beginner mistakes

- Seeing patterns everywhere  
- Drawing trendlines after the move already happened  
- Using the pattern name as the trade thesis  
- Ignoring nearby support/resistance or gaps

## Practice

Find one rising wedge and one falling wedge on historical charts.

For each, force the three questions. Did the “classic” outcome happen? Journal honestly either way — failed patterns teach builders about false positives.

Then do the same for one triangle or flag.

## Builder note

Patterns are excellent “soft-label” material for explanation layers, but weak standalone authority. Learner journals can help distinguish educational value from detector value.
