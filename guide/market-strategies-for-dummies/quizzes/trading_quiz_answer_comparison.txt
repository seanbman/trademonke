SPECIFIC CORRECTIONS

A2 — Bid versus ask

You selected or strongly considered d: “Ask is always below the bid.”

Correct answer: b.

Bid: highest current price a buyer is offering.
Ask: lowest current price a seller is accepting.
Normally, the ask is above the bid.
The difference is the spread.

B6 — Why context matters

This was blank.

A candle can mean reversal, continuation, or ordinary noise depending on:
- its location in the larger trend;
- whether it occurs at a meaningful level;
- the timeframe;
- what happens before and after it.

A hammer at major daily support is not equivalent to a random hammer in the middle of a one-minute range.

C5 — Breakout versus fakeout

Your breakout explanation was reasonably good, but the fakeout definition was missing.

Breakout: price moves beyond a range or structural level and maintains acceptance or continuation outside it.
Fakeout: price briefly breaks the level but quickly reverses back into the prior range.

C8 — Timeframe weight

D — PATTERN SECTION

Double top

Your M-shaped drawing was correct.

Complete answer:
- Shape: two highs near the same level.
- Bias: bearish after a confirmed neckline break.
- Invalidation: price reclaims the neckline and/or makes a clean new high.

The neckline breaking is generally the confirmation, not the invalidation.

Double bottom

Your W shape was correct.

- Bias: bullish after the neckline breaks.
- Invalidation: price loses the lows or the breakout fails back beneath the structure.

Head and shoulders

The drawing appears to show an inverse head and shoulders, with three troughs.

The question asked for a classic topping head and shoulders:
- three peaks;
- middle peak highest;

Triangles

Ascending triangle: flat resistance with rising lows; commonly bullish.
Descending triangle: flat support with falling highs; commonly bearish.

Neither is guaranteed. The opposite-side structural break is a basic invalidation idea.

Flags

2. a tight countertrend consolidation;

I — RISK MANAGEMENT

I7 — Definition of R

“Risk or reward” was not precise enough.

1R is the predefined amount you are willing to lose if the trade reaches its original stop.

Example:
- Entry risk: $50
- Stop hit: −1R = −$50
- Profit of $100: +2R

R lets you compare trades consistently regardless of position size.

I8 — Drawdown

The written response was unclear.

Drawdown matters because:
- it damages both capital and decision-making;
- larger losses require disproportionately larger gains to recover.

For example:
- a 10% loss requires an 11.1% gain to recover;
- a 50% loss requires a 100% gain.

J5 — Process versus outcome goal

The reference to trusting the process and using a checklist points in the right direction, but the distinction and examples were incomplete.

Process goal: something under your control, such as following the checklist and risking no more than 1%.
Outcome goal: a result you cannot fully control, such as making $1,000 this month.
