## At a glance

A **level** is a price area where the market previously reacted strongly enough that you should care when price comes back.

![Support and resistance visual reference](<../../screenshots/WhatsApp Image 2026-07-10 at 3.50.46 PM.jpeg>)

*Figure 4.1. Pattern sheets often mark support and resistance lines explicitly. Use them as visual training wheels, not as magic lines.*

## Levels are places price has “cared about”

A **level** is a price area where the market previously turned, paused, rejected, or repeatedly tagged.

- **Support** — buyers showed up enough that price bounced up (in the past)  
- **Resistance** — sellers showed up enough that price bounced down (in the past)

Past ≠ future. Levels are **candidates**, not guarantees.

## Why levels matter

Without levels, every candle looks equally important. That is the beginner trap.

The real job is not “find any candle that looks dramatic.” The real job is:

1. Find a place that matters.
2. Wait for price to revisit it.
3. Judge what price does **there**.

A weak-looking candle at a very important level can matter more than a huge candle in the middle of nowhere.

## How to mark levels (beginner method)

1. Zoom out (higher timeframe first — e.g. 1h or 4h).  
2. Mark obvious swing highs and swing lows (clear turning points).  
3. Prefer **zones** (a small band) over a single pixel-perfect line.  
4. Zoom in only after the big map exists.

A textbook beginner rule:

> If you need to squint, argue with yourself, or keep moving the line three pixels, the level is probably not obvious enough yet.

## Equal highs / equal lows

When several highs line up, that cluster often matters later. You do not need the advanced liquidity vocabulary yet. Journal it as:

- “equal highs around X”  
- “equal lows around Y”

That wording is already useful to both learners and builders.

## Zones are better than sniper lines

Beginners love exact numbers because exactness feels smart. Markets are messier than that.

A better mindset:

- Use a **band** or **zone**
- Expect slight overshoots
- Care about the **close** and reaction, not just the first touch

This is one reason the later sweep lesson matters so much.

## What we measure later (preview)

The research platform prefers **confirmed pivots** and clear events like:

- touch  
- wick through and reclaim (**sweep**)  
- close beyond (acceptance / break)

You can start by eye; builders will compare your eye to the machine.

## Common beginner mistakes

- Marking ten levels on one screen  
- Drawing lines on every tiny wiggle  
- Using the lowest timeframe first  
- Treating a level break and a level sweep like the same thing

## Practice

Mark 3 support zones and 3 resistance zones on one market.

For each, write:

- Timeframe you used  
- Why you marked it (prior swing? multiple touches?)  
- What would make you say “this level failed” (e.g. strong close through)
- Whether the zone feels **major** or **minor**

## Builder note

If learners consistently mark different zones than the system’s pivot map, that is not just a user problem. It may reveal a product-language mismatch, missing aggregation logic, or poor UI presentation of level confidence.
