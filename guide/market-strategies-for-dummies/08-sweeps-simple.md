## At a glance

A sweep is one of the cleanest beginner concepts in this project: price **goes beyond** a level, but by the close that break **fails**.

*Figure 8.1. Notes connecting liquidity sweeps, change of character, inducement, and long-entry thinking. Beginners only need the simplest layer here: wick beyond, close back inside.*

## Plain definition

A **sweep** (in this project’s careful sense) is when price:

1. Pokes **beyond** a known level with a wick, **and**  
2. **Closes back inside** — the break didn’t stick.

If price **closes beyond** the level, that is often **acceptance / continuation**, not a sweep.

This distinction matters. Internet slang (“stop hunt”) is optional flavor; the measurement is what we keep.

## Why beginners should care

Sweeps near obvious highs/lows are common “trap then reverse” stories. Sometimes they’re real opportunities; sometimes they’re noise. Your job is to combine them with **context**.

Example (long idea sketch):

- Context: uptrend / bullish bias  
- Location: prior lows or equal lows  
- Confirm: wick under lows, close back above  

## The emotional trick of a sweep

A sweep feels dramatic because the wick looks like the break already happened. That is why beginners panic and click too early.

The platform’s discipline is smarter:

- **Wick beyond + close inside** = failed break / sweep  
- **Close beyond** = acceptance / continuation

That one distinction can save months of confusion.

## Journal language (copy this)

```text
Sweep? Y/N
Level:
Wick beyond? Y/N
Close back inside? Y/N
If close beyond instead: label ACCEPTANCE (not sweep)
```

## Common beginner mistakes

- Calling every stop-looking wick a sweep  
- Ignoring the close  
- Looking for sweeps in random places instead of obvious levels  
- Trading every sweep even when context is terrible

## Practice

Find 5 historical examples:

- 3 true sweeps (wick beyond + close inside)  
- 2 acceptances (close beyond)

Write how you felt emotionally looking at the wick — then note how the **close** changed the story.

Then add one extra note:

- Did the sweep happen at a level you had already marked, or did you invent the level afterward?

## Builder note

This lesson is perfect for structured comparison against the system’s sweep rule. When learners and code disagree, you can usually explain it with one of three causes: wrong level, premature read, or close/acceptance confusion.
